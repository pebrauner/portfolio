Berlin wasn't my first Major. I had been in Lima just weeks before, watching the same teams, the same rivalries taking shape. But every event feels different in person. The Velodrom has this low hum between matches, thousands of people breathing and shifting and waiting, and then a teamfight breaks and the whole place erupts and you can feel it deep inside your chest. The LED screens shake a little when the crowd gets loud enough. You notice these things when you're there. The way a player's hand moves to his headset after a bad fight. The way the losing team's monitors go dark one by one. None of that makes it to the broadcast.

I was the only person speaking Portuguese in the entire venue. No other Brazilian journalist, no one from any Portuguese-language outlet. Just me, my recorder, and a credential that felt heavier than it should have. During one of the breaks I went to grab a schnitzel and noticed a guy in the food line wearing an old Seleção jersey, faded and clearly well-loved. We ended up talking for twenty minutes. He was from Minas Gerais, had been living in Berlin for years, and had no idea there was a Dota Major happening until a friend dragged him along. He didn't know any of the teams. He just wanted to watch something loud and competitive. Sometimes that's all it takes.

I was there for four days. I watched EG carry the South American flag further than it had gone in a long time. I watched the Quinn and Remzes rivalry spill off the screen and into the venue. I watched Gaimin Gladiators win their second consecutive Major, something no team had ever done. And between all of that, I managed to sit down with two players from opposite sides of the grand final.

These are those conversations.

<hr class="article-hr">

## Day 2: Nisha

The second day of the playoff stage had three series. Evil Geniuses, with kaffs now coaching, took down OG 2-1 in the lower bracket. Then Gaimin Gladiators dismantled 9Pandas in the upper bracket, a series loaded with tension thanks to the ongoing feud between Quinn and Remzes that, at this point, had escalated well beyond ranked queue trash talk and into in-game tipping and very deliberate eye contact across the stage.

Then Liquid played EG. It wasn't close. In two methodical maps, Liquid ended South America's run and moved on. Afterwards, I caught up with Nisha, Liquid's midlaner, who was preparing for a semifinal the next day.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Sep 2024</span>
<p>A year and a half after this conversation, Nisha lifted the Aegis of Champions at The International 2024 in Copenhagen, sweeping Gaimin Gladiators 3-0 in the grand final. It was Liquid's second TI win as an organization, with two entirely different rosters, a first in Dota history. For Nisha, it was the culmination of years of near-misses: a second-place finish at TI11 with Secret, and multiple runner-up finishes with Liquid throughout 2023. The kid who didn't want to learn maturity from his teammates won the biggest tournament in the world.</p>
</div>

<div class="qa-question">The new patch created a lot of chaos in the scene. Do you think it helped Liquid maintain dominance, or did it force you to rethink everything?</div>

I don't think it helped. I think it created chaos. All of a sudden, we didn't have any strategies and we needed to adapt. I think playing against some teams and in some tournaments helped us a lot. The DreamLeague helped shape the way we think about the game, but overall, I think we're still adapting. I don't think it favors us. Not yet, at least.

<div class="qa-question">Does having your family here add pressure?</div>

I don't really need to worry about them. I think having my friends and family here is only positive.

<div class="qa-question">You're the youngest player on the team. What do you pick up from the more experienced players around you?</div>

I don't think I pick up anything from them. When I was a kid I used to be a little clown, so I don't want to learn anything from them. Experience-wise, I learn from Dota. But maturity-wise? I don't want to learn anything from them.

<div class="qa-question">If you could change one thing about the current patch, what would it be?</div>

I'd reduce the number of neutral camps. It's way too much. If you want to pressure somebody, they just run away to another camp. It's a game of cat and mouse most of the time. Kills give more gold now, but I still think it's too much. Double ancients? Kind of weird. Twenty camps on one side of the map? Kind of weird. I also feel like Roshan should get some status resistance, like 30% at least.

## Final day: tOfu

The last day of the Major had two series. First, 9Pandas versus Liquid in the lower bracket final. The Serbs took map one, but after that, Liquid rolled. The third and final map lasted 21 minutes and 39 seconds, the fastest game of the entire tournament.

And then came the moment that for one team was everything and for another was nothing. For someone to lift the trophy, someone else has to put their hands down. After Liquid lost the grand final 1-3, some of their players didn't get up right away. One of them, I couldn't tell who from where I was standing, just sat there staring at his monitor long after it had gone dark. That empty look, the one where your eyes are open but you're not really seeing anything, just replaying the last five minutes over and over. That image stayed with me longer than the celebration that followed.

Because what followed was Gaimin Gladiators versus Team Liquid, for the third time in a row across three different events. Lima, DreamLeague, and now Berlin. Same matchup, same story. GG won 3-1. With this, they became the only team in Dota 2 history to win consecutive Majors. And they did it in Berlin, tOfu's hometown.

I have some German heritage, and something about watching tOfu win in his own city, in front of his own people, hit a nerve I wasn't expecting. His parents were in the crowd. I could see them. And when I sat down to interview him afterwards, my hands were shaking. tOfu was one of the players I admired most, and now he was sitting in front of me radiating something between exhaustion and pure joy, and I was trying very hard to hold my recorder steady.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Aug 2025</span>
<p>Gaimin Gladiators went on to win all three Majors in 2023, a feat no team has matched since. They then reached back-to-back TI grand finals, losing to Spirit in 2023 and to Liquid in 2024. In late 2024, the org dropped carry player dyrachyo and the team's identity began to fracture. By 2025, coach Cy- was forced out against the players' wishes, Seleri stepped away and eventually retired at 26, and the team withdrew from TI14 over unresolved internal conflicts. Gaimin Gladiators then sued its own roster for over $5 million in damages. The dynasty that started with this Berlin trophy ended in a courtroom.</p>
</div>

<div class="qa-question">This is the first time a team has won back-to-back Majors in Dota history. What does that feel like?</div>

Just incredibly happy that we achieved this, but also relieved in a way. We've been playing Lima, DreamLeague, and now here, one after the other. It feels like we've been playing for two months straight, every day, without stopping. So there's relief now that it's over, but also, to finish on such a high note, because we won again? It's incredible. The crowd here is... my parents are here and they're supporting me so much. My brother, my close friends, several friends I made through Dota. I was looking into the crowd, trying to find where they were sitting. It's beautiful.

<div class="qa-question">You told me two days ago that you'd rather not face Liquid again. Now you have. What do you feel about the series?</div>

Before anything, I was actually happy when Liquid won their match today and we'd have to face them again, just because I really like that team. I'm friends with them, and every time we have to play against each other, whether it's a final or DPC, it's always fun. I feel like we know each other really well. We know their heroes, how they want to play. We're sort of well-balanced, even though we end up winning every time. We always learn a lot from Liquid. When we lost that map, we needed to change something. In a best-of-five you can let one slip, two is too many. But in a way, you have to try new things, and if they don't work, it's your job to adapt. We wanted to try something a little different and got punished for it immediately, which is, you know, good for them. They're a great team, a truly exceptional team. They reached the final for a reason, again and again. You can't play around with them.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Oct 2025</span>
<p>After the collapse of Gaimin Gladiators, tOfu and teammate Ace joined Team Liquid in October 2025. They now play alongside Nisha, the other player interviewed in this piece. The two people I spoke to across two different days at the Berlin Major, from opposite sides of the grand final, ended up on the same team.</p>
</div>

<div class="qa-question">A big part of what you told me the other day was about the crowd. This is your hometown. Your school friends are here, your parents are here. What does it feel like to win in front of them?</div>

For many years of my life, a long time ago, I was living a kind of double life. I had my normal life, working, studying, traveling, whatever, and then I had my gaming life. Over the years I ended up going fully into the player life, and it worked out, these last two years have worked out, but at the same time, it's like... I had my normal friends, my Dota friends, and my team of course. I think now I've finally managed to merge those worlds in a way that works for me. Now I can show everyone what this world I live in actually is, what I actually do, and how good I am at what I do. And it's beautiful to see how everyone has been supporting me in this, even if they're not gamers, or like my parents who have never played anything, or even if they don't know Dota. They support me. They're here for me.

<hr class="article-hr">

*I think about that last answer a lot. Not because it's unique to tOfu, but because it isn't. The double life. Anyone who has ever tried to explain competitive gaming to their parents, to their friends outside the scene, to anyone who doesn't get it, knows exactly what he's describing. That gap between the life people see and the life you actually live.*

*Berlin was the place where, for tOfu, those two lives finally became one. His parents watched him make history from a few rows back, in his own city, and they didn't need to understand Dota to understand what had just happened. I think about the guy in the Seleção jersey too, who didn't know a single team but came anyway because someone he trusted told him it was worth seeing. And I think about the Liquid player who couldn't get up from his chair, staring at a screen that had already gone dark.*

*That's the thing about covering events in person. You don't just hear the answers. You see what's behind them.*
