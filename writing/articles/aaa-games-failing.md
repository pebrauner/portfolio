title: "Why Are AAA Games Failing So Much?"
source: Essay · Jun 2024

---

I have spent north of 3,000 hours playing the Battlefield saga. Mainly with BF4 and BFV, I was, and still am, the guy to rally my squad in Discord and beg to play a few matches. So imagine my horror when I spent $90 for a pre-order copy of the best-most-ultra-version of BF 2042 on Steam just for it to be unplayable. After loving BFV for so much time, a spectacular game, with a phenomenal setting, and absurd graphics (even better than those in 2042 in my humble opinion), I was met with a sequel that just did not work. Plain and simple.

I could rant forever about how much DICE hurt me with Battlefield 2042, but unfortunately the title is far from the only game that failed to deliver in the last few years. Cyberpunk 2077, Fallout 76, Overwatch 2, Redfall, and Forspoken are just a few names in a trend that seems evermore present: multi-million dollar productions that end up failing to live up to fans' expectations.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Apr 2026</span>
<p>The list above has aged in interesting ways. Cyberpunk 2077 is probably the most remarkable redemption arc in modern gaming history. After a catastrophic launch in December 2020, CD Projekt Red spent years overhauling the game from the ground up. The 2.0 update in late 2023 essentially rebuilt core systems like progression, cyberware, and police AI, and the Phantom Liberty expansion with Idris Elba was critically acclaimed. By 2024, Cyberpunk had reached an "Overwhelmingly Positive" rating on Steam and won Best Ongoing Game at The Game Awards. It went from being pulled off the PlayStation Store to being considered one of the best RPGs of the generation. The others on this list were not so lucky.</p>
</div>

But why? What has changed?

<hr class="article-hr">

## The way things used to be

When I was much younger, getting a new game was an event that happened just two or three times a year. I remember playing it for months, replaying it, going for the 100%, diving into multiplayer, enjoying a full, complete experience. In the PS2 and Xbox 360 era, there was no such thing as a "Day-0 patch" or weekly updates. So how did we manage to enjoy things so much back then?

Were the games better, or were we just more naive?

I think the answer lies somewhere in the middle. I was, for a fact, less informed 15 years ago, or at least did not have the same parameters as I have now, but games also were meant to be complete on launch. Patches were few and far between. For something to change radically in a game, something huge had to happen. "Games as a service" were not a thing. The infrastructure needed for that to happen simply was not available, so developers had to include everything they had in a disc and send it out into the world.

Another important factor is the amount of information available at the time. The sources we had about video games were, for most of us, school friends, maybe some weekly 30-minute show on TV, and magazines about gaming in general. News was, at best, very slow. In contrast, nowadays, because of review copies or early access programs, many times we know everything a game has to offer before its launch. For better or worse, the experience is just not the same.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Apr 2026</span>
<p>This observation has only gotten sharper. Since I wrote this, the conversation around information overload has expanded beyond games journalism into AI-generated content, automated trailers, and datamining leaks. By 2025, major publishers started implementing stricter embargo structures precisely because early coverage was shaping launch narratives before players could form their own opinions.</p>
</div>

<hr class="article-hr">

## The indie edge

Just like in movies, every day we see indie games getting a lot of attention for their creativity and ability to use unorthodox ways to tell a story. Games like Buckshot Roulette, Risk of Rain, Hades, Sifu, and Ultrakill, all wildly different and successful, have distinguished themselves through interesting mechanics and good taste in gameplay. All of them were developed by a small team, or even a single developer, and got thousands, if not millions, in sales.

Small games usually have the courage to try new things, and are, for the most part, okay with not being multi-million dollar hits. But when you have an army of devs working day and night to deliver a new game and it doesn't go as expected, the loss is much bigger. And that's precisely why I believe so many of them fail.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Apr 2026</span>
<p>The indie wave hasn't slowed down. In 2024, Balatro, a poker-based roguelike made largely by one person, became a sensation and won multiple awards. Animal Well, another solo-dev project, was one of the most acclaimed releases of the year. Meanwhile, the bigger titles that did succeed, like Astro Bot and Black Myth: Wukong, weren't indie at all, but they shared something with those smaller games: a clear creative identity that players could feel from the first minute. The line between "indie spirit" and budget size keeps getting blurrier. What matters is conviction.</p>
</div>

<hr class="article-hr">

## Why the big ones keep missing

Again, just like in the movies, AAA games are getting blander and more tone-deaf by the day, lacking the edge that used to make them unique. Big publishing studios can't risk upsetting the fans, so they try to develop a game that would please the most amount of people, and end up doing the exact opposite. Most times in life, when everyone likes something, nobody loves it.

That A24-esque feeling we get when playing games like Children Of The Sun, Hi-Fi Rush, Iron Lung, or Outer Wilds is, in my honest opinion, what keeps gaming alive. That not knowing what to expect but, at the same time, enjoying every moment. Gaming has always been about emotion and excitement. So why not try?

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Sep 2024</span>
<p>Two months after I published this piece, Sony released Concord, a hero shooter developed by Firewalk Studios over the course of eight years. It launched on August 23, 2024, never surpassed 700 concurrent players on Steam, and was pulled offline on September 6, just 14 days later. Sony issued full refunds and shut down Firewalk Studios entirely by October. Reports estimated development costs around $400 million. It became, almost overnight, the most dramatic example of everything this article was trying to describe: a massive, expensive production with no creative identity, entering a saturated market with a full price tag while competitors were free-to-play. An internal culture of toxic positivity reportedly prevented critical feedback from reaching decision-makers. Concord is now cited as one of the biggest commercial failures in gaming history.</p>
</div>

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Apr 2026</span>
<p>On the other side of the spectrum, Helldivers 2 launched in February 2024 as a live-service game and immediately hit 400,000 concurrent players on Steam. It proved that the live-service model isn't inherently broken. The difference, once again, was creative identity: Helldivers 2 knew exactly what it was and committed to it fully. The problem was never the model. It was the lack of conviction behind it.</p>
</div>

<hr class="article-hr">

## What I want back

Gaming as a whole has changed a lot in the past few years, but the idea of entering new worlds and changing reality, even if just for a bit, are the factors that keep bringing us back. So please, AAA studios, let the new weird and exciting ideas see the light of day, and take me back to a time when I blew on the back of my memory card in hopes it would work. I want to feel that innocent excitement again, and I know big franchises can achieve it with just a little creativity.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Apr 2026</span>
<p>Since June 2024, the gaming industry has shed an estimated 45,000 jobs across companies like Microsoft, Sony, EA, Epic, Ubisoft, and dozens of others. Studios have been shuttered, projects canceled, and entire pipelines gutted. The human cost of the failures described in this article is not abstract. Every bland, risk-averse AAA title that misses its target doesn't just disappoint fans. It puts real people out of work. The irony is cruel: the industry keeps cutting the very talent capable of making the bold, creative games players are asking for.</p>
</div>

<hr class="article-hr">

*I wrote this in June 2024 for LinkedIn, mostly as a rant from a frustrated player who had been burned one too many times. Rereading it almost two years later, I'm surprised at how much the core argument held up, but also at how much worse things got. When I wrote this, Concord hadn't happened yet. The layoff wave hadn't peaked. The conversation around AI in game development hadn't exploded. What felt like a trend in mid-2024 became a full-blown crisis by 2025. If anything, I was too gentle. The piece was also rougher than I'd like, a little scattered in the middle, and I've tightened the structure here without changing the substance. But the feeling is the same. I still want to be surprised. I still want that moment where a game grabs me and doesn't let go. And I still believe big studios are capable of it, if they stop optimizing for safety and start creating for people who actually play their games.*
