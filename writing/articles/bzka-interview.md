title: "On the Verge: How bzkA Almost Quit Everything and Won the World"

In December 2022, I sat down with Matheus "bzkA" Tarasconi for what turned out to be one of the most honest conversations I've had in esports. He had just been named Best Esports Coach at The Game Awards, had just won Valorant Champions with LOUD, and had just left the organization. He was weeks into building something new with MiBR for the franchise era. He was 27.

This is a trimmed and translated version of that conversation. Some questions have been condensed, and I've added annotations where the world has moved on since we spoke.

<hr class="article-hr">

## The long way around

<div class="qa-question">You've been a CS player, a Valorant player, a world champion coach. Walk us through how you got here.</div>

I started when I was about 12, when I bought my little Counter-Strike CD. My first competitive tournament was CS: Source, at 15. I traveled from Florianópolis to São Paulo with no sponsorship, no nothing. Pure guts. Just because I loved it.

I had a team that actually did pretty well. Top 2 in a bunch of national tournaments. Then we got invited to Dreamhack Valencia, which was massive at the time, one of the biggest international events, all the best teams. We couldn't raise enough money to travel. And in that moment I said to myself: "If I can't play that tournament, I'm wasting my time playing video games." That was it. I lost the fire, lost the drive. I just started playing with friends, for fun.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Jan 2026</span>
<p>Dreamhack Valencia ran its last edition in 2016. The brand itself was absorbed into ESL following the Savvy Gaming Group acquisition of ESL FACEIT Group in 2022. The tournament that almost kept bzkA in CS no longer exists.</p>
</div>

A few years later, in the most completely out-of-nowhere sequence of events, I got called to play the Paladins World Championship. I had downloaded Paladins and was doing well in ranked. One of the guys on the Brazilian team that qualified, Black Dragons, couldn't get his visa. So someone said: "Call bzkA. He's got a visa, he's got competitive experience. Call him, we need a body." And so I went.

I got on that plane having never played a single custom game. Not one practice, not one map walkthrough. Just ranked queue. Total madness. We flew to Atlanta, end of 2016, beginning of 2017, and we got knocked out in groups. Lost every single match. But it was my first international experience, and I hold it very close. I have a lot of love for everyone who was there.

I stayed in the US after that. Some friends called me to play CS and I said: "It's a game I love with all my heart, let's go." I was enrolled in college back in Floripa, had every intention of going home, and then one morning I just woke up and said: "I'm staying." No plan, no safety net. I froze my enrollment, stayed with the guys on pure guts, and Cogu eventually joined the team too. Cogu is someone I consider a master, one of the greatest teachers I've had. I put zakk in that same category, one of the people who mattered most for my growth in CS.

But honestly? I was a mediocre player. I never won anything important. I had some good performances here and there, sure, but nothing special. So I said: "I've got six months left to finish my degree. I'm going back, getting my diploma, because this esports thing isn't really working out either." I didn't have a vision for my future in it anymore.

I kept thinking: "I don't think this is going to happen for me. This isn't my thing. It's not working. I've tried so many times already." So I went back, finished school, got a job. And then, out of nowhere, Riot announces Valorant. A game that was basically a mix of Paladins and CS. And I said: "Bro. This is the perfect game. This is the moment."

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Editorial note</span>
<p>I want to pause here, because something happened in this moment that I think matters more than anything bzkA said. He smiled. Not a polite smile, not a media-trained smile. A real one. Up until this point, he had been telling a story that was, honestly, kind of sad. A dream that wasn't working. From this point on, everything changed. He lit up. Stepping outside the journalist role for a second: it's a genuinely beautiful and motivating thing to hear someone who almost gave up on everything describe the exact moment they decided to bet on themselves one more time.</p>
</div>

I quit my job, quit everything, and dedicated myself fully to understanding the game from day one of the beta. I put together a team, we got Top 3 in Copa Rakin, and I realized the game was special. I was always more of a student than a grinder. Instead of playing 10 ranked games, I'd rather watch 10 VODs. I always ended up filling this tactical role, even when I wasn't the IGL.

## Becoming a coach

When frz invited me to coach Vikings at Champions at the end of the year, I thought: "I have nothing to lose. The Brazilian season is over. These guys are giving me a chance, I'll learn a ton from them, I can contribute with all my hours of study, and who knows, maybe I'll actually like being a coach. Let's give it a shot."

So, in yet another completely random chapter of my life, I packed my bags and flew straight to Berlin. I hadn't coached a single official match in Brazil. Not one. Only scrims. And when I got there and looked around, I was standing on the Champions stage as the coach of a Brazilian team. I thought: "Bro. What is happening right now? What am I supposed to do?" There was this panel under the monitor to switch between screens and stuff, and I had no idea how to use it. I had to call someone over to help me because I literally did not know.

But I loved it. I felt like I could actually help the guys, and I felt like they taught me a ton too. It just felt right. The way you work with people, build strategy, the tactical chess of it all, that was everything I already did as an IGL. Except I was a bot. I was bad. So now I could do the thing I loved without dragging the team down.

After Berlin, I knew one thing clearly: I would only coach if it was with those guys. With Sacy, with saadhak. In my mind, they were the two people who understood Valorant better than anyone in Brazil. Any team I built with them, I'd be a step ahead. Either I'd be with them at the top, or against them at the top.

At that point, the LOUD project was already moving forward with another coach, and I was putting together my own roster to play. I was going to compete no matter what. Then Sacy called me. saadhak called me. They asked: "Have you signed your contract yet?" And I said no. Which, technically, wasn't a lie. The contract was sitting in my hands. I just hadn't signed it because they had written "Free Fire" instead of "Valorant" on the damn thing. So I sent it back: "This is wrong. Fix it and send it back, I'll sign." And in that little window of time, everything changed.

## The Optic thread

I walked into LOUD knowing I was the Plan B. Onur was the first choice. When you arrive in a room under those circumstances, you don't carry the full weight of trust. So I kept my guard low, earned confidence day by day, and only then started coaching the way I believed was right. The day after I arrived, everyone got COVID. I set up my PC on the kitchen counter of an Airbnb and we trained remotely. But it worked.

We won the first VCT undefeated, went to Iceland, finished second. That was a huge surprise for us. What helped massively was starting directly in the playoffs, so every team we faced, we had a mountain of material on them, and they only had footage of us from a month and a half ago. Thank you NiP, who won the Last Chance Qualifier and sent us straight there.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Late 2022</span>
<p>OpTic Gaming, LOUD's great rival throughout 2022, was denied a franchise spot by Riot Games despite being widely considered the best team in North America. The roster disbanded. The core of FNS, Victor, crashies, and coach Chet moved to NRG. yay joined Cloud9. The team that pushed LOUD harder than anyone else simply ceased to exist.</p>
</div>

We made it to the grand final against Optic, and to this day I feel we were not ready to win that tournament. But it ripped open everything we needed to work on, and those were exactly the things we spent the rest of the year grinding.

Then Copenhagen. You know those trips where absolutely everything goes wrong? A player lost his luggage, there were other problems, we arrived destroyed. We lost to KRÜ knowing exactly what they were going to do. We knew their draft, we knew everything, and we still couldn't execute. We lost without playing our game, and that's what hurt the most.

But in the van, heading to the airport, we were still talking about the Optic series. And someone said: "Relax. We're going to come back. The final is going to be against Optic, and we're going to win Worlds." We said this out loud. The cameraman was rolling. We have the footage.

Then Champions comes around. First match: Zeta Division. Win that, and you face... Optic. We looked at each other: "No... you have got to be kidding me. Not these guys. Oh, come ON. Every. Single. Time." Something like 70 or 80 percent of our international matches had been against Optic. But honestly? It was kind of a blessing. We arrived with zero external pressure.

<div class="qa-question">How did you prepare mentally to face them again?</div>

On the mental side, we knew they were the favorites. Even for the whole tournament. But we also knew we were really, really fucking good. And because we knew how good we were, we knew we had a real shot at beating them. The thing is, nobody else knew that. The crowd had just watched us get eliminated in groups in Copenhagen. So we just said: "No matter what happens, when this tournament ends, we'll walk into this little room, just the six of us, and talk. Fuck everything external. We're going in light."

So we went in light. And things clicked. We lost to Optic in the first best-of-three, but that loss might have been the best thing that happened to us all year. It ripped open a bunch of details. Before that, tactical planning was mostly between me, Sacy, and saadhak. After that loss, everyone started showing up to the table. And once everyone was contributing, everything just flowed.

## Coach of the Year

<div class="qa-question">What did winning Coach of the Year at The Game Awards feel like?</div>

I didn't expect it. Not even a little bit. I looked at the list of nominees and these were people with 10-year careers, and I'd been coaching for one year and one month. I said: "There's no way in hell I'm beating these guys. They have their stories, their legacies." And then they said my name. I ran around my apartment screaming. My neighbor came and knocked on my door to ask if I was okay. I popped champagne, celebrated like it was New Year's.

But at the same time, I think it was the closing of a cycle. That award isn't mine alone. It belongs to the entire team, to everything we built that year. I built it with Sacy, with saadhak, with pancada, with Less, with aspas, with Bruno our psychologist, with Artur before him. Every single person who was part of that project. A coach alone doesn't do the work. He can't perform miracles. It was all of us. And if any of you are reading this: that trophy is ours, not mine.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Sep 2024</span>
<p>Within two years of this conversation, the championship roster had fully scattered. Sacy and pancada left for Sentinels. Sacy retired in September 2024. pancada was benched, then returned to LOUD. Of the five, only aspas remains active at the top level, now on MiBR's 2026 roster. bzkA himself coached MiBR from late 2022 through May 2024, finishing 9th in the Americas League in 2023 before parting ways after a difficult start to 2024. He is now Head of Valorant at Los Grandes in Brazil.</p>
</div>

<hr class="article-hr">

*bzkA was one of the best interviews I've ever done. Not because of the championship or the award, but because he was genuinely there. Some people you interview want to get it over with. bzkA made me feel welcome, and I think I managed to do the same for him, and that mutual comfort is what gave this conversation its honesty. I admire his career, obviously, but what I admire most is something smaller than a trophy: that love he has for competition itself. The guy who hates losing at tic-tac-toe. I'm the same way. I don't like losing at checkers, at anything, and I think that's why this piece worked the way it did. I recognized something in him. Rereading it now, three and a half years later, the thing that stays with me isn't anything he said. It's that smile, when he talked about Valorant for the first time. A guy who had tried and failed and tried and failed, describing the exact moment he decided to go again. That doesn't belong to LOUD or MiBR or any roster that has come and gone since. It just belongs to the conversation. I'll carry it with me for a long time.*
