## What is happening to cinema?

Movies in the last few years have been kind of weird.

We are going through a point in time where the way people consume content is shifting. Actually, it has been shifting for a while. The so-called "new media" has influenced the way every piece of content is produced nowadays. If you don't follow a certain structure, you will most likely fail to convey the message you want to the audience you need. This is not a matter of merit or interest, it is just the way social media and today's culture shaped content consumption as a whole. There are, of course, exceptions to this rule, but most of us are not encouraged to try to be the exception. We just want to fit and be a part of the movement. And this, as most things, does not apply only to media. Being the exception is always hard.

Just a few years ago, we were all obsessed with superhero movies. Now, almost every superhero production that comes out seems to flop over and over again. Think Madame Web, think She-Hulk, think Morbius, think Aquaman 2. Multi-million dollar productions that don't seem to understand what the public wants. By the way, I am not saying I understand it either. If I did, I would probably not be sitting in my room writing this right now. But it is a little shocking to see the way things change so radically over just a few years.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Apr 2026</span>
<p>The superhero decline didn't slow down. Since I wrote this, the MCU released Deadpool & Wolverine (which did well, largely on nostalgia and Ryan Reynolds) but the broader trend continued. Marvel pushed Avengers: Doomsday and Secret Wars to winter 2026 and 2027 respectively. DC rebooted entirely under James Gunn's vision. Meanwhile, the movies that dominated 2024 and 2025 had nothing to do with capes: Anora, The Brutalist, Conclave, The Substance. The audience moved on. The studios are still catching up.</p>
</div>

Most films that go really well today are part of two groups:

The first is the **director-driven blockbuster** with hundreds of millions to spend. Think Tarantino, Nolan, Lanthimos, Villeneuve, Scorsese, Spielberg, Anderson. These genius filmmakers have earned enough trust and cultural capital that almost everything they touch becomes an event. These are the mega-productions: Dune, Oppenheimer, Blade Runner, Avatar, Poor Things.

The second is the **A24-esque production**. Low budget, ultra emotionally heavy, almost indie. And in my opinion, this group is the more intriguing one.

We expected those huge director-led productions to go well, since we know the names behind them. But these smaller, more content-heavy films have everything against them and still manage to succeed in ways we thought impossible before. I believe the democratization and availability of film is the big driver behind this. Being able to publish and showcase movies almost everywhere today makes these smaller films reach audiences that would have been unreachable even ten years ago.

I don't expect the huge films from the first group to get as deep and personal as the second, because they serve different purposes. And that's fine. They don't need to compete on the same terms.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Apr 2026</span>
<p>This two-group framework has held up well, and if anything the gap between them has gotten wider. In 2024, the Oscars saw films like Oppenheimer and Poor Things (group one) dominate alongside Anatomy of a Fall and Past Lives (group two). In 2025, the pattern repeated. What's interesting is that the middle ground — the $80-to-$150 million films that aren't auteur-driven and aren't small enough to be scrappy — keeps shrinking. That no man's land is where most AAA failures live, in cinema and in gaming alike.</p>
</div>

## May thy blade chip and shatter.

My favorite saga has always been Lord of the Rings, and my love for medieval fantasy could only ever be matched by my passion for sci-fi.

However, I was born in 2000. Way too late to actually witness The Two Towers on the big screen, or to understand what Lord Vader was planning. So growing up, I always wondered what would be the great next big thing for my generation. Was it the Avengers? Maybe for some. Personally, I never liked Marvel's movies that much. So what would it be?

It is with great honor that I can firmly announce: Dune is the next big thing. Dune Part Two is the Two Towers of my generation.

There are only a few stories out there that can rival the raw epicness of Lord of the Rings. And when you mix a great story with a huge budget, incredible actors (potentially the best ensemble in the world right now), and a genius director, only greatness can come out of it.

Dune Part Two baffled me multiple times during its 2-hour-and-46-minute screening. The sheer scale of the film made me shed tears on more than one occasion, and none of them were the "Marley & Me" kind of tears. No, they were pure, absolute tears of joy and amazement. No other movie I have seen in my life achieved that, and I am so happy one finally did.

No scene in the movie feels stretched out. No dialogue feels vague or pointless. Every shot is stunning. Javier Bardem saying "Lisan Al-Gaib" every five minutes is genius.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Editorial note</span>
<p>I want to be honest here: when I originally wrote this, I had just walked out of the theater. I was high on the experience. Rereading it now, I stand by every word. Dune Part Two earned $714 million worldwide and sits at 92% on Rotten Tomatoes. It was not just a personal revelation. It was a cultural one. Villeneuve did something that felt genuinely rare: he made a blockbuster that trusted its audience to be intelligent.</p>
</div>

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Apr 2026</span>
<p>Dune: Part Three, adapting Frank Herbert's Dune Messiah, has wrapped filming and is set for release on December 18, 2026. The cast now includes Robert Pattinson as Scytale, Jason Momoa returning as Duncan Idaho (as a ghola), Anya Taylor-Joy, and Florence Pugh as Princess Irulan in an expanded role. The story jumps 12 years forward. Paul is Emperor. The Holy War he inspired has killed 61 billion people. The film is described as darker, more political, and more introspective. Villeneuve has confirmed this will be his final Dune film. The first teaser dropped in March 2026, and yes, I cried again.</p>
</div>

It is absolutely great being able to witness Dune Part Two in IMAX, and actually be an adult and understand the things they are talking about. I wish I could have had the same experience with Lord of the Rings. But maybe that's the point. Every generation gets the epic it needs, at the moment it needs it. And I got mine.
