The cavalry has arrived. And with it, a wide range of opinions about the recent launch of Overwatch 2, from the monetization strategy adopted by the developer to the current state of the game's meta.

<p class="img-credit">Image: Activision Blizzard</p>

Let me get this out of the way first: Overwatch 2 is, without a shadow of a doubt, a much better game than its predecessor. Everything is beautiful. The singular level of care poured into each of the game's core aspects is breathtaking. The reworks on several characters, putting an end to the ShieldShootingSimulator™, the introduction of an entirely new game mode (the best one, in my opinion), and the clear definition of roles across five-player teams all added a much-needed dynamism for Overwatch's resurgence as a game that is both fun and competitive at the same time.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Feb 2026</span>
<p>The game eventually dropped the "2" entirely. It's just called <em>Overwatch</em> again. Walter Kong, franchise GM, admitted: "What would I change if we could go back? Probably not call it Overwatch 2." Jeff Kaplan, the original game director, went further in a March 2026 Lex Fridman interview, calling the sequel "one of my biggest mistakes as a creative leader."</p>
</div>

The players clearly felt it. According to [Activision Blizzard's quarterly financial report](https://investor.activision.com/node/35691/pdf), more than 35 million players have already played OW2, including many who are "new to the franchise," with launch month numbers doubling those of its predecessor. It's worth remembering that the original Overwatch was one of the ten best-selling games of all time, with over 50 million copies sold across more than six years on the market. Less than a week after the Overwatch League Grand Finals, in an incredible series that saw the Dallas Fuel take down the San Francisco Shock 4–3, the game should be riding a wave of momentum.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Nov 2023</span>
<p>The Overwatch League shut down permanently after its sixth season. Two-thirds of team owners voted to dissolve the league and collect a $6 million exit payout each, totaling nearly $120 million from Activision Blizzard. The league never recovered from COVID, sponsorship losses tied to the Activision workplace scandal, and dwindling viewership. Competitive Overwatch eventually transitioned to an open circuit run by ESL FACEIT Group.</p>
</div>

So why does it feel like something went wrong?

## The Battle Pass Problem

One of the biggest complaints, and the first major point of this piece, is the battle pass introduced in the transition from the first game to the second. Back then, imperfect as the system was, there was still a way to earn cosmetics for free through loot boxes tied to events or in-game challenges. That's no longer the case. Now, the only way to get new skins and stickers to show off in ranked queues is through microtransactions, and they aren't exactly cheap. The battle pass costs 1,000 in-game credits, roughly equivalent to R$42.00, a price set by the company through coin bundles. If a player reaches the maximum level, they'll unlock 80 in-game cosmetics. However, unlike other games such as Rainbow Six: Siege or Rocket League, OW2's battle pass doesn't give players enough credits within the pass itself to purchase the next season's pass without spending more money.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">2024</span>
<p>This never changed. The OW2 battle pass still doesn't refund enough credits to buy the next one. But the biggest pain point — locking heroes behind the pass — was eventually removed entirely (see below).</p>
</div>

I personally invested my hard-earned money into the battle pass in the name of journalism. And while there really are one or two things I think look nice, I don't believe it's all that.

## Locking Characters Behind a Paywall

The battle pass also introduced something never seen in the first game: characters locked behind progression, and at a painfully high threshold. It takes between 40 and 50 hours of gameplay to reach level 55 of the pass, required to unlock one of the game's new characters, Kiriko. The alternative? Buy the pass and unlock her instantly. Forty hours is a lot of time even for a hardcore player, and more than enough to finish the vast majority of single-player games, if not more than one. On top of that, new players need to complete 150 matches (wins count as two) to unlock the remaining 32 characters in the roster.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Mar 2024</span>
<p>Blizzard reversed this entirely. Starting with Season 10, all heroes became free for all players at launch. No battle pass grind, no paywall. Every previously locked hero was retroactively unlocked. Game director Aaron Keller announced it himself: "Heroes will no longer be unlocked through the Battle Pass."</p>
</div>

This wouldn't be just an inconvenience if it weren't for the fact that Kiriko is currently one of the strongest characters in the game and an essential pick in competitive matches. That lends real weight to the argument that OW2 has become a pay-to-win game, since players who choose not to spend money face a direct competitive disadvantage compared to those who do. The developer tried to counter this claim by arguing that during the development process, steps were taken to reduce hard counters across heroes. Not everyone reached the same conclusion.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Hindsight</span>
<p>The pay-to-win cycle repeated with every new hero. When Mauga launched in Season 8, he was immediately dominant in ranked and locked behind the pass. This pattern continued until Blizzard finally gave up and made all heroes free.</p>
</div>

## An Incomplete Launch

One of the things that bothers me the most is that the game simply isn't finished. Setting aside the bugs and glitches that made the game unplayable during its first days and continue to be a significant part of the experience (Bastion's infinite missiles, Torbjörn's double Overcharge activation), the great promise of OW2 was the PvE campaign, playable in single-player or co-op, which would expand a universe that until now has only been told through cinematics and in-game voice lines. That campaign won't be released until 2023.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">2023–2024</span>
<p><strong>The PvE campaign was never released.</strong> I actually wrote a dedicated follow-up piece on this, <em>"Overwatch 2's Long-Promised PvE Mode Is Cancelled,"</em> published in May 2023 when Blizzard officially pulled the plug on Hero Mode. In short: they scrapped the full campaign, shipped three story missions in Season 6 at a $15 price tag, those sold so poorly that in March 2024 all remaining PvE content was cancelled entirely. The story was eventually resolved through a short audio drama released in late 2025. The "great promise" I was waiting for here never came.</p>
</div>

The early access business model works well for indie or small studios that genuinely need extra funding to cover all the bases for a major launch. That's a stark contrast with Activision Blizzard, a massive company that, in the past, was known for delaying game releases without hesitation to make sure they shipped complete and polished. I obviously prefer they don't release the campaign until it's perfect, but this was, after all, the big promised change. Arguments that all of this should have just been a large patch rather than a "sequel" are far from rare.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">Mar 2026</span>
<p>In his Lex Fridman interview, Jeff Kaplan revealed that Blizzard's then-CFO Dennis Durkin told him OW2 had to hit a specific revenue target, or "we're gonna lay off 1,000 people and it's gonna be on you." Kaplan called it the biggest f-you moment of his career and left the company in April 2021, while OW2 was still in development. The game that shipped was, in his words, "not the Overwatch 2 we planned and announced."</p>
</div>

## So Where Does That Leave Us?

As a fan of the franchise, the universe they've built, the competitive scene, and the company's legacy, I really want OW2 to succeed. Even though I disagree with several of the practices outlined above, OW2 is a game I know I can hop into and have fun with my friends, and I consider that vital for any title. Activision Blizzard still has a lot of work to do, and they need to deliver an incredible campaign if they intend to justify the delay.

<div class="annotation">
<span class="annotation-label">2026</span>
<p>They never delivered the campaign. The justification for the "2" never materialized. In February 2026, Blizzard dropped the number from the title and reset to Season 1. But, to be fair, the game did eventually get better. After Marvel Rivals launched in late 2024 and became a serious competitor, Blizzard responded aggressively: 10 new heroes announced for 2026 (including the fan-favorite Jetpack Cat), a year-long narrative arc called "The Reign of Talon," and a new Perks system. Overwatch recently surpassed Call of Duty and Battlefield in concurrent Steam players. The comeback is real, even if it took years and the pressure of a rival to force it.</p>
</div>

I believe OW2 still has enormous growth potential, and that if the people in charge listen to the community and address the problems outlined here for good, the game will return in no time to the status it once held. For now, I'll keep playing, grinding my battle pass like there's no tomorrow.

I'll see you all on the battlefield, looking ultra-fashion in my mythic Genji skin.
